Escalation

A Writer Can Make
Anything Happen

A. E. Genovese

Increase the danger. Intensify the conflict. Raise the stakes.

These are all familiar refrains to a writer.

We’re talking, of course, about Escalation, which is treated as an effect the writer creates. And why wouldn’t it be?

A writer can make anything happen.

The door opens, an intruder enters, and a gun fires. A secret surfaces, a marriage ends, and a kingdom falls. The dead return, a city burns, and the world ends.

The freedom of authorship is the power to play God. The writer controls every event with absolute authority. Anything that occurs in a story happens only because the writer permits it, and the blank page cannot resist.

And still, a writer can make exactly what they intended happen and lose their story's Escalation in the process.

To illustrate the point, let’s look at a story that begins with its stakes at their apex.

In 12 Angry Men, twelve jurors have to decide whether an 18-year-old boy is guilty of murdering his father. Eleven are ready to convict, but the lone holdout, Juror Eight, asks that a decision ending the boy’s life endure more than a few minutes’ discussion. The stakes are fixed from the beginning. The story doesn't introduce any greater threat and still escalates for almost its entire duration.

The prosecution insisted the murder weapon, a knife, was unique, but Juror Eight lays one just like it on the table. One witness claims he heard the boy threaten his father and a body hit the floor. He says it took him fifteen seconds to reach his door, where he saw the boy fleeing the building. When the jurors retrace his steps, they find it takes them forty-one instead. Another witness says she was in bed when she saw the murder through the windows of a passing train. However, Juror Nine notices marks on Juror Four’s nose from his glasses and remembers seeing the same type on hers. How could she have positively identified the boy when it’s unlikely she was wearing her glasses in bed?

Each doubt weakens the case, making the jurors’ initial certainty harder to sustain. One by one, their votes change until Juror Three is alone in his conviction. The question of whether the prosecution established guilt beyond a reasonable doubt never changes, only the conditions under which it must be answered.

Now let’s pretend a stranger bursts into the jury room with a gun.

Some jurors dive under the table, others cower, one even rushes the gunman. The deliberation stops and a hostage situation begins. The jurors now fight for their own lives and question their captor’s motives instead of deciding the boy’s fate. A new Escalation begins.

Say we change only the gunman's relationship to the deliberation.

He is the victim’s other son, the boy’s half-brother, come to force the jurors to convict at gunpoint. The jurors still must fight for their lives, but now the results of their deliberation relate directly to their plight. The story’s central Escalation continues.

The writer remains completely free, but every new choice is made in a story where other choices have already become true. Juror Eight can return the knife to his pocket. The old man can insist he reached his door in fifteen seconds. The woman can stand by her positive ID. The page will accept every word, but no choice can make it blank again.

The blank page permits anything. The written page contains consequences.

The obvious explanation for this is causality. One event causes another, or changes the conditions under which another occurs. The lie causes the marital strife. The rebellion brings the army to the gates. Juror Eight's refusal to convict continues the deliberation.

But suppose we change only the reason why the gunman enters.

This time, he was just convicted in another courtroom, seizes a marshal’s weapon, and bursts through the wrong door. He has nothing to do with this trial, its evidence, or its vote, other than he knows what it’s like to have his freedom taken by another jury. When he learns that Juror Three is now the lone holdout unwilling to acquit, he points his gun at him and orders him to change his vote.

The other eleven jurors stand between them instead. Their deliberation began with eleven of them demanding Juror Eight surrender his doubt to their certainty. They now refuse to let a fugitive force Juror Three to surrender his certainty to theirs.

The first gunman has no connection to the deliberation. Its Escalation is abandoned while the hostage situation begins another.

The half-brother-gunman enters because of the trial and forces the deliberation to continue at gunpoint. The central Escalation continues with it.

The fugitive-gunman arrives because another jury convicted him, but this jury determines whom he threatens and why eleven men stand in his way.

Causality separates the two strangers from the half-brother. It can't separate the stranger who ends the pressure generated by the deliberation from the stranger who escalates it even further.

What I call "Causal Inheritance" can.

New pressure doesn't have to emerge from the conditions already established. It has to inherit their consequences. Sending a gunman into the room increases the danger, but the deliberation's consequences still have to matter after he arrives. Otherwise, a new Escalation begins.

So then -- an event cannot escalate a story by itself. The writer creates the events through which Escalation becomes visible, but not the principles those events reveal.

Escalation is its own force with its own set of laws. It needs no author.

A husband comes home late without calling and dinner is cold. Work ran late and he couldn’t even think to send a message. His wife snipes, “Work always runs late.” He points to the income his job provides. She points to the kids she raises, the dinners she makes, the dishes she does, the house she keeps running. Soon they’re arguing over whose work matters more and the career she sacrificed. The husband tells her no one asked her to. Next time, he can send her a message ahead of time. The dinner can be reheated. But the sentence cannot be unsaid.

The craft is the evidence.

The Force is the finding.

Escalation is a Narrative Force.

Stories make its principles and progression visible.

It belongs to Life before it belongs to Narrative.

A writer can make anything happen.

But once it happens, the page is no longer blank.
